# CSP Unit 8 – Privacy, Security, and Innovation Simulation: Vocabulary & Concepts

## How to read this

Each lesson lists only what's new: formal vocabulary and the concepts students should walk away with. Timing, prep, links, teaching tips, and teacher dialogue are cut.

- **Vocabulary** is copied word for word from the lesson's formal vocabulary box. Lessons without a box say "No formal vocabulary" and get concepts only.
- **Good to know** terms are defined in passing in the lesson but aren't in a formal vocabulary box. Their basic definitions are my own wording.
- **Key concepts** are condensed from the lesson's overview, objectives, presenter notes, discussion goals, and Check for Understanding.

## Lesson 1: Project – Innovation Simulation Part 1

**Vocabulary**

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Computing Innovation | includes a program as an integral part of its function. Can be physical (e.g. self-driving car), non-physical computing software (e.g. picture editing software), or non-physical computing concepts (e.g., e-commerce). |

**Key concepts**

- This unit runs a role-play simulation. Students take on characters in a "Future School" community (such as a teacher or parent) and speak from that character's point of view.
- Computing innovations are created by people to solve problems, and they've changed how lives are lived. If something doesn't use a program as an important part of how it works, it may not be a computing innovation.
- Benefits of computing innovations include helping schools run more efficiently, giving certain groups a voice, making learning more fun, and saving time and money. Harms come later in the unit.
- For each innovation they research, students record its name, sources, what data it collects, its function and purpose, and why their character would recommend it.

## Lesson 2: Data Policies and Privacy

**Vocabulary:** No formal vocabulary.

**Good to know** (not in the formal vocabulary box)

| Term | Basic definition |
| --- | --- |
| Personally Identifiable Information (PII) | Information that can be used to identify a specific person, such as a name, address, or phone number |
| Biometric Data | Data about a person's body used to identify them, such as a face picture, fingerprint, or voice recording |
| Privacy Policy | A website's or app's explanation of what data it collects and how that data is used |

**Key concepts**

- Computing innovations need data to run, and much of that data is personal or private.
- Biometric data (face images, fingerprints, voice and video recordings) raises the question of whether some data is more private than others.
- Good websites and apps publish privacy policies. Reading them shows what data is actually being collected and how it's used. Check that a policy is recent.
- Users can control the permissions programs have to collect their information, so it's worth reviewing privacy policies and settings.
- Companies collect PII for specific reasons, and knowing those reasons helps you judge data practices.

## Lesson 3: The Value of Privacy

**Vocabulary:** No formal vocabulary.

**Key concepts**

- Sharing data involves tradeoffs: privacy versus convenience (for example, facial recognition) and privacy versus security (for example, the government accessing location data held by third parties).
- A third party is a company or service that holds data about you, such as a phone carrier storing location data.
- In the location-data case, the Supreme Court considered whether the government needs a warrant. That raises the question of how other kinds of third-party data should be handled.
- Students build informed, nuanced opinions on how much private data should be shared to power innovation or ensure security. The goal isn't agreement. It's reasoning tied to specific examples and personal experience.

## Lesson 4: Project – Innovation Simulation Part 2

**Vocabulary:** No formal vocabulary.

**Key concepts**

- Technology can have unintended consequences, so every proposed innovation should be weighed for its benefits and harms.
- The same innovation can be seen as beneficial by some people and harmful by others, or even as both by the same person.

## Lesson 5: Security Risks Part 1

**Vocabulary**

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Keylogging | the use of a program to record every keystroke made by a computer user in order to gain fraudulent access to passwords and other confidential information |
| Malware | software intended to damage a computing system or to take partial control over its operation |
| Phishing | a technique that attempts to trick a user into providing personal information. That personal information can then be used to access sensitive online resources, such as bank accounts and emails |
| Rogue Access Point | a wireless access point that gives unauthorized access to secure networks |

*The lesson plan's vocabulary box misspells this term as "Rouge Access Point." The objectives spell it "rogue."*

**Key concepts**

- Students jigsaw three common security risks (phishing, keylogging, and malware) and then explain them as a group. Rogue access points are also named among the common risks.
- Signs of a phishing attempt: misspelled names, email addresses, or company names; offers that seem too good to be true; requests for personal information; and flashy links asking to be clicked.
- These risks target people as well as technology, so recognizing them is a key defense.

## Lesson 6: Security Risks Part 2

**Vocabulary:** No formal vocabulary.

**Good to know** (not in the formal vocabulary box)

| Term | Basic definition |
| --- | --- |
| Identity Theft | When someone uses a person's stolen personal information to commit fraud, such as making purchases or opening accounts in their name |

**Key concepts**

- Case study: the Equifax breach. Equifax stores private data (like Social Security numbers and bill-payment history) on everyone with a credit score. That data affects whether people can get loans, and at what interest rates, for big purchases like cars and houses.
- Credit data has benefits (companies can make lending decisions, so people can buy things they couldn't pay for outright) and harms (decisions aren't always fair, errors are hard to correct, and a few for-profit companies hold a lot of power).
- Stolen personal data can be used for identity theft: making purchases, draining bank accounts, or ruining someone's financial history.
- The breach came from a software problem combined with human error. Installing an available software update would have prevented it.
- People never agreed to terms of service letting Equifax collect and sell their data, which raises questions about how such companies, and the government, should handle private data.
- Citizens can push for stronger data protections.

**Vocabulary:** No formal vocabulary.

**Key concepts**

- This is mainly a work day to finish individual innovation one-pagers that fit the team's shared vision.
- Computing innovations can harm society, the economy, or culture even when their creators didn't intend it.

## Lesson 8: Protecting Data Part 1

**Vocabulary**

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Decryption | a process that reverses encryption, taking a secret message and reproducing the original plain text |
| Encryption | a process of encoding messages to keep them secret, so only "authorized" parties can read it |
| Public Key Encryption | pairs a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption. The sender does not need the receiver's private key to encrypt a message, but the receiver's private key is required to decrypt the message |
| Symmetric Key Encryption | involves one key for both encryption and decryption |

**Good to know** (not in the formal vocabulary box)

| Term | Basic definition |
| --- | --- |
| Caesar Cipher | Encryption that shifts every letter of the alphabet by the same number of places |
| Random Substitution Cipher | Encryption that matches each letter of the alphabet to a different, randomly chosen letter |
| Cracking Encryption | Trying to decode a secret message without knowing all the details of the cipher |

**Key concepts**

- A Caesar cipher is easy to crack: find a short word and try shifts until it becomes English, and then the rest decodes. With a computing tool, it takes seconds.
- A random substitution cipher is harder to crack than a simple shift, but computing tools still help with decryption.
- Symmetric encryption uses one key shared by the sender and receiver, so they have to exchange it.
- Asymmetric (public key) encryption uses two keys, one public and one private. The sender and receiver never need to meet to exchange a key.
- The modern Internet is built around public key encryption.

## Lesson 9: Protecting Data Part 2

**Vocabulary**

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Computer Virus Scanning Software | protects a computing system against infection |
| Multi-factor Authentication | a system that requires at least two steps to unlock protected information; each step adds a new layer of security that must be broken to gain unauthorized access |

**Key concepts**

- A password alone is single-factor authentication: one layer based on something you know.
- Multi-factor authentication combines at least two of three kinds of factors, and ideally all three:
    - something you know (a password),
    - something you have (a phone that receives a code),
    - something you are (biometrics like a fingerprint or retina scan).
- MFA is much more secure than a password alone, but it adds extra steps and time, and its factors can be lost or stolen (for example, a stolen phone or lost SIM card).
- Two ways to protect against viruses: use virus scanning software and keep all software updated.
- Data protection is a moving target. New threats keep appearing, so updates have to be ongoing.
- The lesson ends with an AP-style stimulus question about an innovation's data, benefits and harms, effects, and security or privacy concerns.
